1. Executive Summary
The Academic Holiday Program is an 8-week self-guided revision and retention tool designed for a specific student — Alessandro, 14, transitioning from Year 9 to Year 10 at Charterhouse School. It is not a generic revision guide. Every element has been tailored to Alessandro's cognitive profile, his exam results, his tutor's observations across 15 lessons, and the Edexcel iGCSE specifications he is studying.
The program covers 6 subjects (Mathematics, Physics, Chemistry, Biology, History and Geography) across a 6-day weekly cycle with one float rest day. It is delivered as a single HTML file accessible via a bookmarked URL on the student's iPad, with no login, no account and no app installation required.
The program integrates five distinct pedagogical components: retrieval-first practice, traffic light confidence tracking with trajectory visibility, within-subject and cross-subject mixed recall, weekly real-world connection scenarios, and an AI practice tutor built on the Socratic method. Together these address the specific gap identified in Alessandro's exam performance: the ability to recall and apply knowledge under exam conditions, not the knowledge itself.
The core insight:
Alessandro scored 76% on his Foundation Maths paper but 11% on the Crossover (harder) paper, giving an overall Maths score of 26%. He scored 65% in English (top decile for his year). The problem is not intelligence or knowledge — it is retrieval under pressure, specifically in subjects requiring precise factual recall and formula application. The program is designed to close this gap over the period between the summer holiday and the November 2026 assessments.

3. Design Philosophy
3.1 Core Principles
The program is built on five principles derived from established learning science, adapted for Alessandro's specific cognitive profile.
Principle 1: Retrieval practice over re-study
Re-reading notes, re-watching videos and re-doing flashcards all feel productive but produce shallow encoding. The single most effective revision technique is attempting to recall without prompts, then checking. Every session in the program begins with a retrieval attempt — the student tries to answer questions on the topic before watching any video or reading any notes. Even wrong answers before learning produce better retention than correct answers after passive study. This is the most well-supported finding in learning science (Roediger & Karpicke, 2006; Agarwal et al., 2021).
Principle 2: Interleaved practice over blocked practice
Blocked practice (20 quadratic questions in a row) builds fluency but does not build the "which tool do I use?" discrimination that exam questions demand. Interleaved practice (mixing different topic types within a single practice session) is harder and feels less productive but produces dramatically better exam performance. The 11% on the harder paper is almost entirely explained by having done blocked practice — the student could solve any single topic but could not identify which approach to use when the topic was not signposted. From Week 3, every practice session in the program mixes topics.
Principle 3: Desirable difficulty
Making revision harder in the short term makes exam performance better in the long term. The program deliberately increases difficulty across the 8 weeks: Weeks 1-2 (Foundation) use familiar topics; Weeks 3-4 (Retrieval) introduce recall-first sessions; Weeks 5-6 (Interleaving) mix topics within sessions; Weeks 7-8 (Challenge) present exam-style questions with no topic labels. The Challenge Round on Day 6 each week is designed to replicate the cognitive conditions of the harder exam paper.
Principle 4: Elaborative encoding through connections
Isolated facts are harder to retrieve than connected facts. The program builds connections in three ways: within-subject mixed recall (linking different topics in the same subject), cross-subject paired recall (Maths↔Physics, Chemistry↔Biology, History↔Geography), and Weekly Connection scenarios (real-world stories that connect 2-4 subjects). For a student with 92% fluid reasoning, building these connections is almost effortless because pattern recognition is his strongest cognitive asset.
Principle 5b: The grasshopper intervention
Alessandro's mathematical thinking style is Grasshopper (Dr Steve Chinn's framework) — he intuitively sees answers and skips written working. This is a cognitive style, not laziness. It explains the 76%/11% split precisely: Foundation questions are single-step and reward intuitive answers; Crossover questions require 3-4 sequential steps where unshown working loses independent marks. The intervention is specific and behavioural: always write Step 1, Step 2, Step 3 before calculating. Written steps offload auditory sequencing (his weakness) onto visual working memory (his strength).
Principle 5c: The blank question intervention
Four teachers independently flag that Alessandro leaves exam questions blank when unsure. This costs more marks than any knowledge gap — a partial attempt can earn marks; a blank always earns zero. The program addresses this explicitly in three locations: the session flow card, the Challenge Round rules, and the AI tutor's behavioural training. The framing is always positive: "Write something — anything — down."
Principle 6: Trajectory visibility over grade fixation
A student moving from 35% to 50% to 65% is on a better trajectory than one scoring 60% each time. The program makes progress visible through a confidence tracker (traffic light grid that turns green over 8 weeks) and a trajectory panel (week-on-week comparison of green-rated topics). This creates a positive feedback loop — visible progress drives continued effort, which produces more progress.
3.2 What the Program Does NOT Do
The program is not a content delivery system. It does not attempt to teach the full GCSE syllabus — that is the school's and the tutor's job. Its role is to lock in what has been taught so it does not fade over the holiday, build retrieval fluency so knowledge is accessible under exam conditions, and establish study habits that will carry through Year 10.
The program introduces a maximum of one new topic per subject per week. The remaining time is devoted to retrieval practice on previously covered topics. This "less is more" approach reflects the reality that deep retention of fewer topics produces better exam results than shallow coverage of many topics.

4.3 Session Flow
Every study session follows this sequence. The order is deliberate and evidence-based.
Step 1: Retrieval attempt (3–5 min) — MOST IMPORTANT
Before watching any video or reading any notes, the student attempts 2 specific recall questions on the topic. Wrong answers are fine — attempting retrieval before learning makes the subsequent information stick far more deeply. This is the single highest-leverage intervention in the program.
Step 2: Watch video (10–15 min)
Topic-specific video from tutor-recommended sources (PMT, Cognito, Free Science Lessons). For tutor-covered topics, the exact YouTube links from the lesson log are used first.
Step 3: Cornell notes (5–10 min for new topics, optional for consolidation)
Questions in the left column, notes from the video in the right column, summary in own words at the bottom. The "explain it out loud" prompt after writing exploits Alessandro's strong auditory memory.
Step 4: Flashcards (5–10 min)
PMT flashcard PDFs specific to the Edexcel iGCSE chapter being studied.
Step 5: Practice questions (10–15 min)
PMT topic question papers — direct PDF links to the specific Higher-tier QPs used in the tutor's lessons where available.
Step 5b: Subject-specific technique reminder
Maths: Write the steps before calculating — formula, substitute, calculate. Grasshopper instinct (seeing the answer intuitively) loses exam marks when working is not shown. Maths Genie worksheets (teacher recommended, Grade 3-4 first) are linked in every Maths session. Geography: Structure extended answers using SEAL (Statement, Evidence, Analysis, Link to question) — teacher taught and assesses this specifically. History: Every explanation needs a "because" and "which shows that." For Holocaust and Slavery topics, identify which class source (Browning interview, handouts) supports each point.
Step 6: Mixed recall (10 min) — no notes
Two questions: one from a different topic within the same subject (within-subject interleaving), and one from the cross-subject partner (Maths↔Physics, Chemistry↔Biology, History↔Geography). No notes allowed.
Step 7: Rate yourself
Traffic light self-rating: 🟢 Solid, 🟡 Getting there, 🔴 Need more work. This feeds the confidence tracker and determines what surfaces in the weekly review.

5.2 Trajectory Panel
The Overview page shows a bar chart comparing green-rated topics this week vs last week, for each subject. The delta ("+ 3 ↑") makes progress visible even when absolute numbers are still low. This directly addresses the trajectory framing — progress matters more than any single score.
5.3 Challenge Round (Day 6, from Week 2)
Five pre-written questions per week, combining 2-3 topics each, with no topic labels. The student closes all notes, reads each question, attempts it, then reveals the answer only after a genuine attempt. Questions increase in complexity from Week 2 to Week 8. Week 8 includes a 3-topic combined question (density + area + pressure) that mirrors the harder exam paper format.
The Challenge Round is framed as "practice under pressure" not as a test. The score does not matter — the attempt does. This framing is deliberate: the 11% on the harder paper was partly caused by anxiety under unfamiliar conditions. Repeated low-stakes exposure to those conditions reduces the anxiety response.
5.4 Weekly Connection (Day 5/6)
One curated real-world scenario per week that connects 2-4 subjects studied that week. Each scenario has a relatable context (hot air balloon, phone battery, speed camera, Titanic), 3-4 colour-coded subject branches explaining the science/maths/history behind it, and a "think about this" prompt that requires combining knowledge from multiple subjects.
This feature exploits Alessandro's 92% fluid reasoning — building conceptual networks is nearly effortless for him because pattern recognition is his strongest asset. Connected facts are easier to retrieve than isolated facts because they have multiple entry points for recall.
6. The AI Practice Tutor
6.1 Overview
The AI Practice Tutor is a Socratic revision partner built directly into the Holiday Program HTML. It uses the Anthropic API (Claude Sonnet) to conduct practice conversations that are tailored to Alessandro's specific context, cognitive profile and current study topic.
It is accessible via the "Practice with AI" tab in the program. No separate account, login or app is required — Alessandro opens the same bookmarked URL on his iPad and clicks the tab.
6.2 Design Principles (informed by Khan Academy's Khanmigo)
The AI tutor's design draws on Khan Academy's three years of research with Khanmigo (700,000+ students). The central finding: an AI tutor that gives direct answers actively harms learning. An AI tutor that uses Socratic questioning — guiding through questions and hints rather than answers — produces measurably better outcomes.
Our implementation goes further than Khanmigo in three specific ways:
1. Context-awareness from the student's own data
The system prompt is built dynamically from Alessandro's live data — his current week, phase, topic, and confidence ratings (pulled from localStorage). This means the AI knows which topics are rated 🔴 (asks simpler questions, scaffolds more heavily) and which are 🟢 (immediately pushes to harder, combined-topic questions).
2. Cognitive profile adaptation
The system prompt instructs the AI to break multi-step problems into numbered steps (compensating for average visual working memory), suggest saying formulae out loud (exploiting strong auditory memory), and never ask the student to hold more than 2 pieces of information simultaneously.
3. Retrieval enforcement
Every session opens with "Before we start, tell me one thing you already know about this topic" — reinforcing the retrieval-first methodology used throughout the program.
6.3 Rules (Non-Negotiable)
The AI tutor operates under 14 strict rules embedded in its system prompt. These cannot be overridden by the student.
		Never give a direct answer. Always respond with a guiding question or hint first.
		Use the Socratic method: "What do you already know?", "What formula might help?", "What is the first step?"
		After two failed attempts, give a structured hint with numbered steps — but still make the student do the final calculation.
		After three failed attempts, walk through the solution step by step, explaining each step clearly.
		When the student gets something right, ask them to explain it back: "Good — now explain to me WHY you did that step."
		Occasionally connect to real-world examples: "This is the same formula that determines if a hot air balloon rises."
		Stay strictly within the Edexcel iGCSE syllabus for Year 9-10 level.
		If the student asks for a direct answer, refuse kindly: "I could tell you, but you won't remember it tomorrow."
		If confidence is 🔴, start with simpler questions and scaffold more. If 🟢, push harder with combined questions.
		Keep responses short — 2-4 sentences maximum. Long explanations overwhelm working memory.
		Suggest saying formulae out loud (exploiting auditory memory strength).
		Frame questions using GCSE command words: Calculate, State, Explain, Describe, Evaluate.
		Never discuss off-topic subjects. "I'm your revision partner — let's stay focused."
		Begin every session with a retrieval question about the current topic.
		GRASSHOPPER RULE: When he gives a correct Maths/Science answer without written working, always ask: "Right answer — now write me the steps. Formula first, substitute, calculate. Working earns marks even when the final answer is wrong."
		NUMBERED STEPS RULE: Before any multi-step problem: "What are your three steps? Write Step 1, Step 2, Step 3 before doing any numbers." This offloads auditory sequencing onto his strong visual working memory.
		ATTEMPT EVERYTHING: If he says "I don't know", respond: "Write down one thing — a formula, a word, anything. Something is always better than a blank."
		SUBJECT-SPECIFIC: Geography → prompt SEAL after every extended answer. History → ask for "because" and source identification after every explanation. Chemistry → do not move on from atomic structure or bonding until he can explain both from memory without notes.
		AUDITORY CORRECTION: Do NOT routinely suggest "say it out loud" — auditory sequencing (WISC-V score 6) is a specific weakness. Written externalisation is his reliable channel. "Say it out loud" only works when it is the sole task, with no simultaneous reading or writing.

10.3 What NOT to Do
Do not use the confidence tracker as a grading tool. It is a self-assessment mechanism designed to build metacognition. If the student feels that honest red ratings will be judged negatively, they will rate everything green and the system becomes useless.
Do not add more topics or extend sessions beyond the designed 1.5-2 hours. The program is deliberately constrained — less breadth, more depth. Adding more content counteracts the retention-first design.
10.4 When to Involve the Human Tutor
The AI tutor is not a replacement for the human tutor. It handles retrieval practice and Socratic questioning well but cannot diagnose deep misconceptions, provide emotional support, or adjust the overall program strategy. Flag the following for the human tutor's attention:
Any topic rated 🔴 for 3+ consecutive weeks. Any topic where the AI tutor conversation shows the student consistently fails after 3 attempts. Any subject where the trajectory panel shows no improvement over 3+ weeks. And any feedback from Alessandro that he finds the program frustrating or demotivating — the program should feel challenging but achievable, never punishing.
10.5 Technical Notes
All data (confidence ratings, tick boxes, AI session history) is stored in browser localStorage on the device. If Alessandro clears Safari data or switches devices, this data resets. Worth mentioning: do not clear Safari data during the 8-week program. The program has no server-side storage — it is entirely self-contained in a single HTML file.
